# Agent 2 — What Goes Wrong: Recurring Clause Types That Burn People

Research for the Redline hypothesis. Question: which clause types actually cause harm, ranked by how often they show up in complaints? This document treats Redline as unvalidated and reports evidence for and against it.

## 1. Summary (5 lines max)

Nonpayment/late-payment, undisclosed junk fees, forced arbitration/class-action waivers, auto-renewal traps, and security-deposit disputes each have real, sourced volume behind them — from federal regulators (FTC, CFPB) or large practitioner surveys (Freelancers Union, EPI, Zillow/Rent.com). Non-compete harm is well documented but is currently in legal limbo (FTC rule vacated in court, Sept 2024–2025), which weakens its case as a near-term wedge. IP assignment, personal guarantees, and scope creep are widely described as painful in qualitative/practitioner sources but I could not find independent counts or percentages for them — treat those as directional, not measured. The evidence does NOT show that people fail because they can't read/understand clauses in isolation; several sources (arbitration awareness gap, EPI relief-rate data, personal-guarantee "signed without understanding") point instead to a comprehension gap *plus* leverage asymmetry — the clause is often non-negotiable regardless of whether the signer understood it. No source in this research directly tested whether a plain-English summary tool changes outcomes.

### Wants vs. needs — notes

- F1/F2 (auto-renewal, nonpayment): sources point to what people actually needed being **structural/legal fixes** (easier cancellation flow enforced by regulation; legal payment-enforcement mechanisms like NY's Freelance Isn't Free Act) rather than better comprehension of the contract at signing time. This is a mild counter-signal to a "read it for you" product — the harm shows up at enforcement/friction time, not at the reading-comprehension stage.
- F3/F4 (arbitration): this is the clearest "comprehension gap" case in this research — 75% didn't know the clause existed, under 7% knew it blocked lawsuits. A plain-English flag ("you are giving up your right to sue or join a class action") maps directly onto this gap. But note F3 also shows that even when known, arbitration terms are usually non-negotiable take-it-or-leave-it — knowing doesn't necessarily change the outcome, only the decision to sign or walk away.
- F5 (security deposits): the want (documentation, itemized deductions) is largely a process/evidence problem, not a contract-language problem — landlords already have to disclose this in many states; the gap is landlords not doing it, not tenants not understanding the clause.

## 5. Signals That Contradict or Weaken the Redline Hypothesis

- **Leverage, not comprehension, may be the binding constraint.** F3/F4 shows most consumers don't know their contract contains an arbitration clause, but even fully informed consumers largely cannot negotiate it away — these are standard-form, non-negotiable terms across nearly the entire market (majority-to-two-thirds of US households, 81 of Fortune 100 companies). Telling someone "this clause is risky" doesn't help if there's no alternative counterparty to sign with instead. This directly weakens the "drafted counter-offer per clause" feature for consumer ToS/arbitration — there's often no one to send the counter-offer to.
- **Regulators are already moving on the highest-volume items.** Auto-renewal (FTC click-to-cancel rule, finalized Oct 2024) and rental junk fees (CFPB attention, Sept 2024) are both subject to active or recent federal rulemaking/reporting. If regulation solves the auto-renewal and junk-fee problem structurally (mandating easy cancellation, banning certain fees), the market opportunity for a per-document reading tool on those specific clause types could shrink over time — though enforcement timelines and coverage gaps (state-level leases, e.g.) may keep it relevant longer.
- **Freelancer nonpayment — the biggest-volume finding here — is not really a "clause you didn't understand" problem.** F2 shows even among freelancers who use a written contract, a third are still paid late and 10% aren't paid the agreed amount at all; the fix pursued was legislative (Freelance Isn't Free Act), not comprehension. This suggests the actual killer feature might be enforcement/collection tooling, not clause explanation.
- **Non-compete evidence is strong but legally unstable.** The FTC's own ban was vacated in court; as of this research, noncompetes are enforceable in most US states. A product framing non-competes as "the thing Redline warns you about" is building on a moving legal target, not settled harm.
- **No source tested "would a plain-English tool have prevented this."** Every source in this research documents the outcome (nonpayment, fee, lost deposit, arbitration loss) but none tests whether better upfront comprehension of the clause would have changed the signer's behavior or the outcome. This is a genuine evidence gap for the core Redline value proposition — I found market pain but not causal evidence that comprehension tooling fixes it.

### Ranking method

Ranks 1, 2, 5 are anchored to a single named, dated, quantified source I directly fetched (FTC, CFPB) — the strongest evidentiary basis in this research. Ranks 3-4 rest on a secondary compilation site (centerjd.org, rapideyeinspections.com) that names its own primary sources (EPI, CFPB 2015 study, Zillow, Rent.com, Roost) but which I did not independently re-fetch for every underlying number — medium-high confidence. Ranks 6-10 are lower confidence: either a single low-authority source with unverified claims (scope creep), a regulatory record without a raw complaint count (non-compete), or purely qualitative/anecdotal legal-blog consensus with no numbers at all (personal guarantee, IP assignment, unilateral ToS changes). This ranking is **not** derived from counting mentions in my own sample of forum threads — I did not sample forums in this research — so it should be read as "confidence-weighted synthesis of published sources," not a market-share ranking of complaint volume across all clause types. Where a source gave a number, that number is quoted; where it didn't, the ranking reflects how many independent voices flagged the issue and how directly they described consumer/worker harm.
